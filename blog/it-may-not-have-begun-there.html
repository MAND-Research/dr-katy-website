It May Not Have Begun There

Why the moment a symptom appears is rarely where it began.

She asked me if my patient’s symptoms were tied to a specific event.

The answer could change what kind of care my patient would have access to moving forward. If I said yes, definitely, she would be much more likely to have that care covered. If I said no, I could sabotage her chances of receiving the full spectrum of care she may need.

The truth is, I do think her symptoms were triggered by the event. I could not ignore the timing or the severity of the disruption. But the deeper truth is that we will never really know for sure.

This is not only a problem with insurance or medicine or law, though it shows up clearly there. It starts with the human mind. We want cause and effect. We want to know what happened, why it happened, who is responsible, what should have been done differently, and what should happen next. Then we build systems around that need. We ask bodies to fit into clean stories because clean stories are easier to remember, easier to defend, easier to bill for, easier to approve or deny.

But bodies do not always work that way.

I think about this a little like riding a bike. When a bike has enough forward momentum, a small turn of the wheel is usually not a problem. The bike and the rider can adjust. There may be a wobble, but the motion itself helps with correction. When the bike has slowed down enough, though, the same small turn can knock the whole thing off balance. The turn is part of the fall, but it is not the whole explanation.

Bodies can be like this. A person can compensate for a long time. Hormones shift, sleep gets worse, inflammation rises, digestion changes, stress accumulates, pain increases, and the body keeps finding ways to keep the person going. Then something happens. Maybe it is a major event. Maybe it is a smaller stressor landing at the wrong time. Maybe it is simply the point where the system can no longer keep up. What becomes visible in that moment may look sudden, but it may not have begun there.

This is part of what I learned studying Chinese medicine and naturopathic medicine at the same time. Chinese medicine, especially, trained me to think in movement, cycles, relationships, timing, excess, depletion, stagnation, and repair. At first, it felt like it was breaking my brain. I was used to looking for straighter lines: this causes that, this symptom means this, this lab points to this intervention. But after enough time in that way of thinking, I started to see how things fit together while they were moving.

That does not mean every answer needs to be broad or complicated.

Endometriosis is a good example. It affects more than one system. It can touch pain, digestion, hormones, mood, sleep, inflammation, energy, relationships, work, and a person’s basic sense of whether they can trust their body to get through the day. Supporting those systems can change what daily life feels like.

But seeing the whole system does not mean pretending there is no specific pathology. With endometriosis, there may be tissue growing where it should not be, inflammation, severe pain, and hormonal forces that keep pushing the body out of balance. In that context, a specific intervention may be exactly what is needed. Excision surgery, hormonal birth control, or other hormone-modulating therapies are not a failure of systems thinking. They may be what gives the system enough room to recover.

So I am not interested in replacing one oversimplified story with another. I do not want “everything is connected” to become another way of avoiding precision. Sometimes the narrow lens is the right one. Sometimes the lab gives the clearest next step. Sometimes the diagnosis points to the treatment. Sometimes the medication, surgery, nutrient, or protocol is the thing that helps.

But the narrow lens is not the only lens.

A spreadsheet can show a number moving up or down. It cannot always show the relationship between the number and the life around it. It cannot show what the body has been compensating for, what changed before the symptom appeared, what still has momentum, or where the system has started to lose its ability to correct.

That is what I mean when I say the body is a system, not a spreadsheet. Data is useful. Clear diagnoses are useful. But clarity is not the same as certainty, and the cleanest story is not always the most honest one.

When she asked me whether her symptoms were tied to the event, I told her the truth. I do see a strong correlation between when her symptoms started and what happened, though I cannot prove it, and neither can anyone else. But I was certain of something else, and I told her that too. This set of symptoms means she will need more care going forward, not less.

That was the most honest answer I had. It was not a clean cause and effect, but it was a clear read of where her body is and what it needs.

Dr. Katy Morrison

Naturopathic doctor and acupuncturist specializing in midlife women's health.
